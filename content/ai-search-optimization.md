# AI Search Optimization in 2026: AEO, GEO and LLMO Explained

AI search optimization stopped being a separate discipline in 2026. Google builds AI Overviews and AI Mode on the same Search index, the same ranking systems and the same snippet eligibility rules as everything else, and it states plainly that there are *no additional technical requirements* to appear in those features ([AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). What changed is not the retrieval machinery — it is the **unit of competition**. Classic SEO competes for a ranked list entry that a human clicks. AI search competes for a *quotable passage* inside a synthesized answer that may never produce a click at all.

Three concrete consequences follow, and they define this guide:

1. **Access and retrieval are now policy decisions.** `robots.txt` has to distinguish AI crawlers that produce citations from AI crawlers that only train models, and the industry is converging on a standards-track vocabulary for that ([IETF AI Preferences working group](https://datatracker.ietf.org/wg/aipref/about)).
2. **Structure beats keyword matching.** The original GEO research found that adding statistics and quotations measurably increased source visibility in generative answers while *keyword stuffing performed worse than doing nothing* ([GEO: Generative Engine Optimization, arXiv:2311.09735](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735)).
3. **Measurement is fragmented by design.** Google now reports AI-feature impressions separately in Search Console ([Generative AI performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139)), but mentions and citations inside third-party answers have no official reporting surface at all, so you build your own panel.

Everything below is grounded in primary documentation and published measurement, with the date each source was last revised. Where the industry is guessing — as it does about `llms.txt` adoption and AI visibility "scores" — that is labelled as such.

**The scale.** At Google I/O 2026, Google reported that AI Overviews had passed **2.5 billion monthly active users** and that AI Mode had "already surpassed 1 billion monthly active users" in the year since launch ([Google I/O 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/sundar-pichai-io-2026)). Google published its dedicated optimization guide on **15 May 2026**, announced by John Mueller on the Search Central blog ([Search Central blog post](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2026/05/a-new-resource-for-optimizing)). These are not niche features any more; they are the primary interface for a billion-plus monthly users on one platform and 2.5 billion on another.

## How do AI answer engines actually retrieve and cite sources?

The pipeline is not magic; it is a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system with a product wrapper. Google describes its own implementation directly:

> "**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: A technique (also known as grounding) used to improve the quality, accuracy, and freshness of AI responses by relying on our core Search ranking systems to retrieve relevant, up-to-date web pages from our Search index. Our systems then review the specific information from those retrieved pages to generate a more reliable and helpful response, showing prominent, clickable links to relevant web pages that support the information in the response." — [Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)

The second mechanism is the one that matters most for content strategy:

> "**Query fan-out**: A set of concurrent, related queries generated by the model to request more information and fetch additional relevant search results to address the user's query. For example, if the original user's query is 'how to fix a lawn that's full of weeds', fanout queries might include 'best herbicides for lawns', 'remove weeds without chemicals', and 'how to prevent weeds in lawn'." — [same source](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)

Google's public AI Mode documentation says the same thing in product language: it "divides your question into subtopics and searches for each one simultaneously" ([AI Mode in Google Search](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/16011537)). AI Overviews and AI Mode "may use a 'query fan-out' technique — issuing multiple related searches across subtopics and data sources," and while the response is being generated, "our advanced models identify more supporting web pages," which is why AI Mode can display "a wider and more diverse set of helpful links" than a classic search ([AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)).

**Why fan-out changes what you need.** A classic SERP evaluates your page against one query. Fan-out evaluates it against a family of related sub-questions, each retrieved separately, and your page competes for a slot in every one of them. A page that answers only the literal question is eligible for one sub-question; a page that comprehensively covers the topic cluster is eligible for several. This is the mechanical reason topical breadth beats keyword repetition in AI search.

### What the retrieval layer actually looks like

Independent research on the black-box behavior of generative search engines confirms the mechanics and quantifies them. Comparing Google's organic results against AI Overviews, Gemini, GPT-4o-Search and GPT-4o-with-search across six datasets, researchers found:

- On average, **53% of the domains an AI Overview consults are not in the top 10 organic results** for that query (27% are not in the top 100) — the AI layer reaches source pools the organic SERP never surfaces ([arXiv:2510.11560, *Characterizing Web Search in the Age of Generative AI*](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11560v1)).
- The same study measures how many pages each engine actually consults: **8.6 for AI Overviews, 8.5 for Gemini, 4.1 for GPT-Search, and 0.4 for GPT-4o-with-search-tools**. A model with search disabled answers mostly from its parameters; a model with search enabled reads roughly a dozen pages per query.
- Source diversity differs sharply: 38% of organic-result domains sit in the top 1,000 most-visited sites, versus 34% for AI Overviews and 35% for GPT-with-search.

The practical reading: AI engines retrieve from a **wider, flatter, less authority-concentrated** pool than a top-10 organic SERP. Rank position alone is a poor predictor of citation. The same study also found that on ambiguous queries (a name shared by several people, a technical term used in different disciplines), generative engines "sometimes are not able to cover all the different aspects," and that organic results provided better coverage — a genuine argument for keeping a clean classic SEO foundation in parallel.

Anthropic's retrieval engineering posts make the same point from the other side: their Contextual Retrieval method — prepending document-specific explanatory context to each chunk before embedding and before BM25 indexing — reduced failed retrievals by **49%**, and by **67% when combined with reranking** ([Anthropic, *Contextual retrieval in AI systems*](https://www.anthropic.com/research/contextual-retrieval)). A passage stripped of its context — a floating statistic with no subject, no unit, no date — is exactly the chunk that retrieval loses.

### What triggers an AI answer at all?

Not every query gets an AI answer, and the distribution is heavily skewed by intent and phrasing. Ahrefs analysed **146 million desktop SERPs** from September 2025 across 86 keyword traits ([What Triggers AI Overviews?](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overview-triggers/)):

| Trait | Share of queries that trigger an AI Overview |
| --- | --- |
| All keywords (baseline) | 20.5% |
| Informational intent | 21.4% |
| Commercial intent | 4.3% |
| Transactional intent | 2.1% |
| Navigational intent | 0.9% |
| Question-form queries | 57.9% |
| Queries of 7+ words | 46.4% |
| Single-word queries | 9.5% |
| Non-branded queries | 24.9% |
| Branded queries | 13.1% |
| Science category | 43.6% |
| Health category | 43.0% |
| Shopping category | 3.2% |
| News category | 15.1% |
| Local searches | 7.9% |

The headline number: **99.9% of keywords that trigger an AI Overview are informational**, and AI Overviews are **1.9x more likely on non-branded queries** than branded ones. (The source reports news twice with different figures — 6% in its summary and 15.1% in its category table — so treat the news number as approximate; the intent, commercial and transactional figures are unambiguous.) Academic research points the same way from user-behaviour data — longer queries, queries beginning with a question word, and queries containing both a noun and a verb are all more likely to produce one ([arXiv:2608.04831, Pew Research Center](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.04831v1)).

**What this means commercially.** If your traffic comes from short branded and transactional queries, AI Overviews barely touch it. If your traffic comes from informational "how/what/why" queries at 7+ words, it is the primary battleground — and it is the battleground where the click does not happen.

### Which pages get cited?

Citation is not evenly distributed, and the distribution is not a function of domain quality. Ahrefs tracks the most-cited domains in Google AI Overviews monthly; the November 2026 edition ranks YouTube at **24.5% mention share**, Reddit at **18.1%**, Facebook at **12.4%**, Google at **8.6%**, Instagram at **5.4%**, Quora at **4.4%** and Wikipedia at **4.4%** ([The 50 Most-Cited Websites in Google AI Overviews](https://ahrefs.com/blog/most-cited-domains-ai-overviews)). Every one of the top seven is a platform with massive first-party user-generated content.

That is the uncomfortable finding of AI search: platforms whose content is already structured as short, attributable, self-contained statements — a forum answer, a video transcript, a wiki paragraph — win on citation volume. No editorial strategy on a small site matches that output. What it *does* mean is that your target format should be one short, attributable, self-contained statement per claim, because that is the unit that gets lifted.

## What actually differs from classic SEO?

Classic SEO and AI search optimize the same substrate — your indexable pages and their quality signals — for different outputs. This is the cleanest statement of the difference:

| Dimension | Classic SEO | AI search |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output unit | A ranked URL | A passage attributed to a URL |
| Winning condition | Position for one query | Eligibility across many fan-out sub-queries |
| Competitive field | ~10 results on one SERP | Dozens-to-hundreds of retrieved sources |
| Click economics | Click is the conversion | Click is one of two outcomes; being cited is the other |
| Content shape | Long-form can hide a good answer | The extractable statement must be findable |
| Primary signal | Links, authority, relevance, experience | Same core ranking + non-commodity specificity + quotable evidence |
| Measurement | Impressions, clicks, position, CTR | Impressions, citation share, mention share, referral share |
| Failure mode | Ranking loss | Absence from the answer — invisible in Search Console |

The row that causes the most damage in practice is **click economics**. Google is explicit that the two coexist: AI Overviews "are only shown when our systems determine that it is additive to classic Search, and as such, often don't trigger," and "AI Mode and AI Overviews may use different models and techniques, so the set of responses and links they show will vary" ([AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). But when they do trigger, they take the click.

### The click-through evidence

Four independent studies, three methodologies, one consistent direction.

**Peer-reviewed behavioural data.** A Pew Research Center study of 900 US adults' real browsing (91,121 Google Search page visits, 68,879 distinct queries, March 2025) found that about **18% of searches generated an AI Overview**, and that:

- Clicks to sources cited inside AI Overviews were **very rare — about 1% of visits** to an AI Overview page.
- Users clicked a result link on **15%** of SERPs without an AI Overview, but only **8%** of visits to SERPs with one.
- Users **ended their browsing session** on 26% of pages with an AI Overview versus 16% without.

These differences held in a mixed-effects logistic regression controlling for panelist random effects and query attributes that make AI Overviews more likely to appear ([arXiv:2608.04831](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.04831v1)). The authors are careful to note the design is observational, so it cannot establish causation — but it is the strongest behavioural evidence available and it points the same way as the commercial data below.

**Search-console-based CTR modelling.** Ahrefs re-ran a study using **300,000 keywords** (150,000 with an AI Overview, 150,000 informational without) and aggregated Google Search Console CTR, comparing December 2023 (pre-AIO) with December 2025. The presence of an AI Overview now correlates with a **58% lower average click-through rate for the position-1 page** ([Ahrefs, *Update: AI Overviews Reduce Clicks by 58%*](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-reduce-clicks-update)). The effect is graded by position:

| Position in results | Click-through rate impact |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | -58.0% |
| 2 | -50.8% |
| 3 | -46.4% |
| 4 | -38.8% |
| 5 | -32.6% |
| 6 | -30.5% |
| 7 | -29.7% |
| 8 | -28.8% |
| 9 | -29.7% |
| 10 | -19.4% |

**Segment-level data at scale.** Seer Interactive tracked **5.47 million queries and 2.43 billion organic impressions across 53 brands** from January 2025 to February 2026. Their 2026 update found the decline *stopped* — organic CTR on AIO-present queries rebounded from a floor of **1.3% in December 2025 to 2.4% in February 2026**, while non-AIO queries rose from 2.8% to 3.8% ([Seer Interactive, *AIO Impact on Google CTR: 2026 Update*](https://www.seerinteractive.com/insights/aio-impact-on-google-ctr-2026-update)). Two of their findings are the most actionable numbers in this section:

- **Being cited in an AI Overview delivers +120% more organic clicks per impression** than not being cited — but still underperforms a no-AIO query by **-38%**. Citation is a relative advantage inside an AI answer, not a return to the pre-AIO baseline.
- AIO prevalence varies enormously by intent: **informational 36%, commercial 8%, transactional 5%**. Within informational queries, comparison queries trigger an AIO **95.4%** of the time and question-format queries **85.9%** of the time.

**Market-level zero-click data.** Using Similarweb's cross-device clickstream panel, SparkToro found that **68.01% of Google searches ended without a click** in the first four months of 2026, up from 60.45% in 2024 — the fastest two-year acceleration in a decade ([SparkToro, *In 2026, Less than One Third of Google Searches Still Send a Click*](https://sparktoro.com/blog/in-2026-less-than-one-third-of-google-searches-still-send-a-click)). The same analysis found only **0.34%** of searches reached AI Mode between January and April 2026 — the zero-click shift so far is driven overwhelmingly by AI Overviews and instant answers, not by AI Mode. AI tools themselves send **less than 1%** of all traffic out to the open web.

**What the operators say.** Google notes that when people click from a results page with AI Overviews, "these clicks are higher quality (meaning, users are more likely to spend more time on the site)" ([AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). Independent data agrees on engagement quality if not on volume: visitors arriving from AI search engines spend **68% more time** on sites than organic visitors, because the assistant has already filtered for intent ([SE Ranking, *AI traffic grew 16x from 2024 to 2026*](https://seranking.com/blog/ai-traffic-research-study)).

The strategic conclusion is not "SEO is dead." It is that **traffic and influence have decoupled**. A page can now be the most-cited source in an answer and generate no measurable visit. That is why this guide separates three measurements that most teams still collapse into one: impressions, citations, and referrals.

## How do you decide which AI crawlers to allow?

This is the part where most published advice is wrong, because it flattens "AI crawler" into one category. Every major vendor now separates bots by *purpose*, and each purpose has a different business consequence for you.

### The taxonomy that matters

| Bot | Operator | Purpose | Effect of blocking | Recommendation if you want citations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `OAI-SearchBot` | OpenAI | Indexes pages for ChatGPT search results | "Sites that are opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers, though can still appear as navigational links" ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots)) | **Allow** |
| `ChatGPT-User` | OpenAI | Fetches a page when a user or Custom GPT asks for it | "Because these actions are initiated by a user, robots.txt rules may not apply" | Allow (may not be honoured anyway) |
| `GPTBot` | OpenAI | Crawls content that may be used to train foundation models | "Disallowing GPTBot indicates a site's content should not be used in training" — no effect on search | Your choice; block to opt out of training |
| `Claude-SearchBot` | Anthropic | "Navigates the web to improve search result quality for users" | "prevents our system from indexing your content for search optimization" ([Anthropic](https://support.anthropic.com/en/articles/8896518-does-anthropic-crawl-data-from-the-web-and-how-can-site-owners-block-the-crawler)) | **Allow** |
| `Claude-User` | Anthropic | Fetches pages in response to a user question | Disabling "prevents our system from retrieving your content in response to a user query" | Allow |
| `ClaudeBot` | Anthropic | Collects web content that could contribute to model training | Signals that future materials should be excluded from training datasets | Your choice; block to opt out of training |
| `PerplexityBot` | Perplexity | "Designed to surface and link websites in search results on Perplexity. It is not used to crawl content for AI foundation models" ([Perplexity](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/resources/perplexity-crawlers)) | Site does not appear as a linked result in Perplexity search | **Allow** |
| `Perplexity-User` | Perplexity | Fetches a page when a user asks Perplexity a question | "Since a user requested the fetch, this fetcher generally ignores robots.txt rules" | Allow (may not be honoured) |
| `Googlebot` | Google | Classic Search index — **also the source AI Overviews and AI Mode retrieve from** | Your page cannot be retrieved for AI features at all | **Allow** — non-negotiable |
| `Google-Extended` | Google | Controls use of Google-crawled content for training future Gemini models and for grounding in Gemini Apps / Vertex AI | "Google-Extended does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search nor is it used as a ranking signal in Google Search" ([Google's common crawlers](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers)) | Independent of AI Overviews — see below |

### The Google-Extended misconception, resolved

`Google-Extended` is the single most misreported token in AI SEO. Three facts from Google's own documentation settle it:

1. It is **a product token, not a distinct HTTP user agent**. "Google-Extended doesn't have a separate HTTP request user agent string. Crawling is done with existing Google user agent strings; the robots.txt user-agent token is used in a control capacity" ([Google's common crawlers](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers)).
2. It governs **only** Gemini training and Gemini/Vertex grounding — "Google-Extended does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search nor is it used as a ranking signal in Google Search."
3. Blocking `Googlebot` is what removes you from AI Overviews and AI Mode, because those features retrieve from the classic Search index. Blocking `Google-Extended` does **not**.

So a site can disallow `Google-Extended` (opt out of Gemini training) and still be fully cited in AI Overviews. Many published guides conflate the two, which is why "block Google-Extended" advice quietly destroys AI Overview visibility for readers who follow it literally.

There is also a separate, newer lever. Google ships a **Search generative AI control** in Search Console (Settings → Search generative AI) that opts a property in or out of AI Overviews and AI Mode outright. Excluding a site means its content is no longer eligible as an input to generate an AI response or preview in those features, and the change generally takes effect within one to two days ([Search generative AI control](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16908024)). That control "isn't used as a ranking or inclusion signal affecting other parts of Search," and it does not affect AI training — for that, Google points back to `Google-Extended`.

Note the two-tier structure this creates, which is the right way to think about the whole surface:

- **Acquisition control** — `Allow`/`Disallow` decides whether a bot may fetch your page at all.
- **Usage control** — a separate signal decides what the fetcher may *do* with what it fetched.

### A workable robots.txt policy

The policy below is the one this site publishes, and the reasoning generalizes. The principle: **allow everything that can cite you or refer traffic to you; block only what takes content without giving anything back.**

```
# Classic search — mandatory, and also the source for AI Overviews / AI Mode
User-agent: Googlebot
Allow: /
User-agent: Bingbot
Allow: /

# AI search / retrieval — these produce citations and referral traffic
User-agent: OAI-SearchBot
Allow: /
User-agent: Claude-SearchBot
Allow: /
User-agent: PerplexityBot
Allow: /
User-agent: Applebot-Extended
Allow: /
User-agent: Google-Extended
Allow: /

# AI user-initiated fetchers — a human asked for this page
User-agent: ChatGPT-User
Allow: /
User-agent: Claude-User
Allow: /
User-agent: Perplexity-User
Allow: /

# AI training crawlers — no citation, no referral
User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /
User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /
User-agent: CCBot
Disallow: /
User-agent: anthropic-ai
Disallow: /
User-agent: Amazonbot
Disallow: /
User-agent: Bytespider
Disallow: /
User-agent: Diffbot
Disallow: /

Sitemap: https://example.com/sitemap.xml
```

Note that `ClaudeBot` (training) and `Claude-SearchBot` (search) are **different tokens** and conflating them either loses your Claude citations or quietly re-enables Anthropic training. Anthropic's documentation states that its bots "respect 'do not crawl' signals by honoring industry standard directives in robots.txt," and it additionally supports the non-standard `Crawl-delay` directive for rate control ([Anthropic crawler documentation](https://support.anthropic.com/en/articles/8896518-does-anthropic-crawl-data-from-the-web-and-how-can-site-owners-block-the-crawler)).

Two operational notes that cause real failures:

- **Propagation is not instant.** OpenAI states it "can take ~24 hours from a site's robots.txt update for our systems to adjust" ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots)); Perplexity says each setting "works independently, and it may take up to 24 hours for our systems to reflect changes" ([Perplexity](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/resources/perplexity-crawlers)). A robots.txt change is a two-week project, not a same-minute toggle.
- **WAFs block bots before robots.txt is read.** Cloudflare and AWS WAF configurations need explicit allow rules keyed on both user agent *and* published IP range, or bot-management rules will challenge the very bots you permitted. Perplexity publishes exact WAF guidance, including the Cloudflare caveat that "Bot Fight Mode can't be skipped with WAF custom rules" ([Perplexity WAF configuration](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/resources/perplexity-crawlers)). Cloudflare's own AI Crawl Control product now provides per-crawler allow/block plus robots.txt-compliance monitoring and a GraphQL analytics API ([Cloudflare AI Crawl Control](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-crawl-control/)).

### The standard that is being written right now

The per-vendor token approach has a known weakness: it requires the publisher to know, and guess at, every vendor's taxonomy. The IETF's **AI Preferences (aipref) working group** is standardising a way to express usage preferences independently of the bot, so the preference travels with the content rather than the crawler identity.

Two standards-track documents went to the IESG on 31 August 2026:

- **`draft-ietf-aipref-vocab`** defines a vocabulary of usage categories. The current vocabulary has two core categories: *Foundation Model Production* (training or fine-tuning a model) and *Search* (using an asset in a search application that directs users to the location the asset was retrieved from, presented with a reference to that location and verbatim excerpts) ([draft-ietf-aipref-vocab-05](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-ietf-aipref-vocab-05.txt)). Notably, the Search category explicitly permits internal processing for ranking and presentation, including training of the models used in the search application — a deliberate carve-out so that opting out of AI *training* does not accidentally opt you out of being *findable*.
- **`draft-ietf-aipref-attach`**, by Gary Illyes (Google) and Martin Thomson (Mozilla), defines the transport: a `Content-Usage` HTTP header field and a `Content-Usage` directive in `robots.txt`, explicitly updating RFC 9309 ([draft-ietf-aipref-attach-05](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-ietf-aipref-attach-05.txt)). The draft separates acquisition from usage: "The Allow and Disallow rules determine what resources can be crawled... This creates a two-stage arrangement that distinguishes acquisition and usage."

The proposed syntax is one line:

```
User-agent: *
Allow: /
Content-Usage: train-ai=n
Content-Usage: /ai-ok/ train-ai=y
```

The draft is careful about its own status: these are Internet-Drafts, "work in progress," not RFCs, and preferences are explicitly *not* a security mechanism. But the direction is clear, and the failure semantics are already specified in a way that matters operationally — conflicting `Content-Usage` preferences on identical paths resolve by the vocabulary's reconciliation process, "which differs from the Allow/Disallow rules, where a conflict leads to the more permissive option."

**Practical advice for 2026:** implement the vendor-token policy today, because that is what the vendors actually honour. Add `Content-Usage` lines as a forward-compatible annotation only if you are comfortable being early — no major vendor is documented as honouring them yet.

## Is `llms.txt` worth implementing?

This is the question with the most bad advice on the internet, so it deserves a careful answer rather than a yes or no.

### What the specification says

`/llms.txt` is "a proposal to standardise on using an `/llms.txt` file to provide information to help agents use a website," authored by Jeremy Howard, first published 3 September 2024 and revised to **v2 on 10 August 2026** ([llmstxt.org](https://llmstxt.org/)). It is explicitly a proposal, not a standard — the spec page invites community input through an open GitHub repository and Discord.

The v2 format is a Markdown file with a required structure: an H1 with the site name (the only required section), a blockquote with a short summary, optional detail paragraphs, then H2 sections containing link lists where each entry is a Markdown hyperlink with optional notes. A convention called "Optional" holds secondary links an agent can skip when it needs a shorter context. The file "stays small enough to fit in context. The detail lives behind the links, and is fetched only when needed."

v2 also proposes serving a clean Markdown version of every page at the same URL — `.md` appended (`page.html.md`) or the extension replaced (`page.md`) — and advertising them with standard link relations: `rel="alternate" type="text/markdown"` for the Markdown version and `rel="describedby"` for the `llms.txt` that covers the page, usable either as HTML `<link>` elements or as an HTTP `Link:` response header.

Adoption, per the spec's own changelog, is real: "thousands of sites publish an `llms.txt` file," documentation platforms generate one automatically, Chrome's Lighthouse "audits sites for one as part of its agentic browsing checks," and the AI labs publish one for their own developer documentation — OpenAI, Anthropic and Gemini all do.

### What the evidence says about whether it is *fetched*

The honest answer is: rarely, and much less often than the marketing implies.

Google's position is unambiguous and repeated. Its optimization guide lists `llms.txt` among the tactics that are **not** needed: "You don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, markup, or Markdown to appear in Google Search (including its generative AI capabilities), as Google Search itself doesn't use them" ([Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). Search Engine Journal's reporting adds that John Mueller has compared `llms.txt` to the discredited keywords meta tag, and that at Search Central Live APAC, Gary Illyes and Amir Taboul confirmed Google is not pursuing it ([SEJ, *Google's llms.txt Guidance Depends On Which Product You Ask*](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/googles-llms-txt-guidance-depends-on-which-product-you-ask/575431)).

Independent log evidence agrees. A 30-day server-log census of one site's machine-readable files, from 27 July to 15 August 2026, recorded **13,442 fetches of `/robots.txt`, 2,841 of an OKF content bundle, and only 157 of `/llms.txt`**. Restricted to Google, Anthropic and OpenAI, the split was 1,150 robots.txt fetches, 23 OKF fetches and **4 `llms.txt` fetches — none of them from OpenAI**. Of those four, three were Googlebot, and "the three Googlebot fetches were followed by no page request from the same IP addresses," i.e. they went nowhere ([dejan.ai crawler census](https://dejan.ai/blog/crawler-census-robots-llms-okf)). The census's own caveat is the right one to carry forward: these are counts of file fetches, "not renders, citations, or answer appearances."

So: **`llms.txt` is not currently an indexing mechanism for any major answer engine, and the one primary measurement of it in the wild shows minimal fetching.**

### But the same Google publishes an audit for it

Chrome's Lighthouse added an **Agentic Browsing** category in version 13.3, promoted to default configuration, with audits covering WebMCP integration, agent-centric accessibility, cumulative layout shift, and `llms.txt` ([Lighthouse agentic browsing scoring](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/agentic-browsing/scoring)). The `llms.txt` audit "flags the pages if a server error occurs when attempting to retrieve the `llms.txt` file. If the file is not provided by the server (resulting in a 404), the audit is marked as Not Applicable (N/A), as providing the file is optional at the moment" ([Lighthouse llms.txt audit](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/agentic-browsing/llms-txt)).

The `llms.txt` audit doc describes the file as "an emerging convention used to provide a machine-readable summary of a website's content, specifically designed for LLMs and AI agents," and adds the operational rationale: "Without this file, agents may spend more time crawling the site to understand its high-level structure and primary content."

**The reconciliation is that these are two different products with two different goals.** Google Search says `llms.txt` is unnecessary for *Search visibility*. Chrome's Lighthouse says it is useful for *browser-agent readiness* — an agent navigating your site directly, not a search index fetching you for synthesis. A site can legitimately have no interest in the first and real interest in the second.

### The decision

| If you are… | Then |
| --- | --- |
| Optimizing for Google AI Overviews / AI Mode | `llms.txt` has **no effect**. Skip it and spend the effort on content quality. |
| Serving documentation or a reference API | Implement it. Documentation platforms generate it automatically, and it is where the spec's own use case lives. |
| Publishing a personal or professional profile that a browser agent might navigate to answer "who is X" | Implement it. It is cheap, it is a curation surface you control, and it is auditable. |
| Operating at scale with many pages | Implement it only if you can keep it accurate. A stale `llms.txt` is worse than none. |
| Unsure | Implement it as a 2 KB static file plus Markdown twins. It costs an hour and it is the one machine-readable file where *you* decide what an agent reads first. |

A minimal, spec-compliant example for a personal site:

```markdown
# Ivan Weiss van der Pol

> Senior AI and automation engineer. Agent orchestration, LLM platforms,
> AI-assisted QA and data engineering. Paraguay — open to remote.

Ivan builds production agentic systems: multi-agent fleets, MCP server
integrations, LLM cost and quality controls, and the CI gates that keep
them honest. Before that he led QA for an FDA-cleared Class II medical-device
platform and built Python automation that cut manual testing effort by 60%.

## Start here

- [Résumé](https://example.com/cv/): ATS-readable HTML résumé, employment history, quantified outcomes.
  markdown: https://example.com/cv.md
- [About](https://example.com/about/): Long-form professional narrative with a verifiable timeline.
  markdown: https://example.com/about.md
- [AI search optimization guide](https://example.com/writing/ai-search-optimization/): How answer engines retrieve and cite sources, with primary sources.
  markdown: https://example.com/writing/ai-search-optimization.md

  markdown: https://example.com/ai-crawler-policy.md

- [Selected work](https://example.com/work/): Project case studies with problem, method, result, metrics.
  markdown: https://example.com/work.md
```

The spec's own testing advice is the right acceptance criterion: "Test your file by asking an agent questions about your content, giving it only your `llms.txt` as a starting point."

## What else does "agent-readiness" require?

`llms.txt` is one surface of a broader shift: agents consuming websites as *interfaces* rather than as documents to summarise. web.dev's guidance on building agent-friendly sites is the primary reference, and it is explicit that agents work from a machine-readable representation rather than a rendered page ([web.dev, *Build agent-friendly websites*](https://web.dev/articles/ai-agent-site-ux)).

Agents view a site in three primary ways, and you have to be legible in all three:

1. **Screenshots** — a vision model reads the rendered page. Helpful for visual cues, slow and token-expensive, so it is a fallback.
2. **HTML** — the agent reads the DOM and understands nesting, hierarchy, attributes and raw data strings. "If a 'Buy Now' button is inside a product container, the agent assumes that button belongs to *that* specific product."
3. **The accessibility tree** — "a browser-native API [that] distils the DOM into what's most important: roles, names, and states of interactive elements… For an AI agent, it functions as a high-fidelity map that ignores the visual 'noise' of CSS." Modern agents combine modalities, "us[ing] the DOM and accessibility tree to get a clean, structured list of interactive elements, and then cross-reference that with a visual rendering."

The practical rules that follow:

- **Avoid ghost elements.** Transparent overlays that hide interactive elements may be discarded by visual analysis even when they appear transparent.
- **Use semantic HTML.** `<button>` and `<a>` over `<div>` with click handlers; if you cannot, provide the correct `role` and `tabindex`. Agents recognise the former as interactive.
- **Label every input.** "Add the `for` attribute on `<label>` tags to link them to inputs. This helps the AI agent understand the purpose of a field."
- **Meet a minimum hit area.** Interactive elements needed to complete an action should be "visible and larger than 8 square pixels, to avoid being filtered out in the model's visual analysis."
- **Keep the layout stable.** "Agents that take screenshots will likely be confused if your website layout is constantly shifting."
- **Expose tools if you have them.** WebMCP is a proposed standard where a site declares its own actions to agents via JavaScript or HTML annotations, so agents "interpret these tools faster and more accurately than with standard actuation" ([Chrome WebMCP](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/webmcp/)).

Google's own guide points at the same territory from the search side, noting that "browser agents may access your website to gather the data they need to complete these tasks, such as analyzing visual renderings (like screenshots), inspecting the DOM structure, and interpreting the accessibility tree," and that protocols like the Universal Commerce Protocol "are emerging that will allow Search agents to do more" ([Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)).

The unifying insight: **the accessibility tree is the agent's document model.** Every accessibility defect is a retrieval defect, and vice versa. This is why accessibility work is not a side quest in AI search — it is the technical foundation of machine readability.

## Does structured data still matter for machine readability?

Short answer: yes for entity resolution and rich results, no as an AI-citation lever — and the second half is Google's explicit position.

Google states: "**Overfocusing on structured data**: Structured data isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add. However, it's a good idea to continue using it as part of your overall SEO strategy, as it helps with being eligible for rich results on Google Search" ([Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)).

So the honest framing is two separate jobs:

| Job | Structured data role | Primary standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Being cited inside an AI answer | **Not a lever.** Google's retrieval and ranking systems decide; no markup boosts citation. | — |
| Being eligible for rich results | **Direct and decisive.** | Google Search Central structured data docs |
| Entity resolution (who is this?) | **Indirect but real.** Consistent `sameAs` and `@id` make your entity unambiguous across sources. | schema.org |

The rules for using it well are Google's general structured data guidelines ([General structured data guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies)), and the ones that matter most for machine readability:

- Use **JSON-LD** — Google's recommended format (the other two supported formats are Microdata and RDFa).
- **Never mark up content that is not visible to readers.** "Don't mark up content that is not visible to readers of the page."
- **Don't misrepresent ownership or purpose** — "Don't use structured data to deceive or mislead users. Don't impersonate any person or organization."
- **Be specific.** "Try to use the most specific applicable type and property names defined by schema.org."
- **Be complete.** Required properties are eligibility gates; recommended properties improve quality.
- **Watch for manual actions.** A structured data manual action "means that a page loses eligibility for appearance as a rich result; it doesn't affect how the page ranks in Google web search."

For entity work specifically, schema.org defines `sameAs` as the "URL of a reference Web page that unambiguously indicates the item's identity. E.g. the URL of the item's Wikipedia page, Wikidata entry, or official website" ([schema.org/sameAs](https://schema.org/sameAs)). The `Organization` type itself is used on more than 10 million domains based on monthly aggregations from Google's web index ([schema.org/Organization](https://schema.org/Organization)).

A minimal, correct entity block for a professional profile:

```html
<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@graph": [
    {
      "@type": "Person",
      "@id": "https://example.com/#person",
      "name": "Ivan Weiss van der Pol",
      "url": "https://example.com/",
      "jobTitle": "Senior AI & Automation Engineer",
      "knowsAbout": [
        "Agent orchestration",
        "Large language models",
        "Retrieval-augmented generation",
        "AI search optimization",
        "Quality engineering"
      ],
      "sameAs": [
        "https://github.com/IvanWeissVanDerPol",
        "https://linkedin.com/in/ivanweissvanderpol"
      ]
    },
    {
      "@type": "WebPage",
      "@id": "https://example.com/cv/#webpage",
      "url": "https://example.com/cv/",
      "isPartOf": { "@id": "https://example.com/#website" },
      "about": { "@id": "https://example.com/#person" }
    }
  ]
}
</script>
```

Three details do the work: a stable `@id` on the person node, `sameAs` pointing at third-party profiles that already corroborate the identity, and `about` linking pages to that entity. Every page on a site should reference the *same* `@id` — otherwise each page describes a different, disconnected person, and the entity cannot be resolved.

## What content actually gets quoted?

This is where the primary research is unambiguous and where most published "AEO hacks" come apart.

### The GEO paper: what moved the needle

The GEO paper (Aggarwal et al., KDD 2024) formalised generative engines as a pipeline of generative models plus a search engine, and measured nine content-modification strategies against a no-optimization baseline on GEO-bench, a 10,000-query benchmark. It proposed two visibility metrics: **Position-Adjusted Word Count** (words attributed to your source, exponentially decayed by citation position) and **Subjective Impression** (a GPT-3.5-scored blend of relevance, influence, uniqueness, position, count, probability of clicking and diversity).

The results, from Table 1 of the paper ([arXiv:2311.09735v3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09735v3)):

| Method | Position-Adjusted Word Count | Subjective Impression | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No optimization (baseline) | 19.3 | 19.3 | — |
| Keyword Stuffing | 17.7 | 20.2 | **Worse than baseline on the primary metric** |
| Unique Words | 20.5 | 20.4 | Negligible |
| Easy-to-Understand | 22.0 | 20.5 | Helps |
| Authoritative | 21.3 | 22.9 | Helps |
| Technical Terms | 22.7 | 21.4 | Helps |
| Fluency Optimization | 24.7 | 21.9 | Helps |
| Cite Sources | 24.6 | 21.9 | Helps |
| Quotation Addition | **27.2** | **24.7** | Strongest |
| Statistics Addition | 25.2 | 23.7 | Strong |

The summary in the paper's own results section: "our top-performing methods, Cite Sources, Quotation Addition, and Statistics Addition, achieved a relative improvement of **30-40% on the Position-Adjusted Word Count metric and 15-30% on the Subjective Impression metric**," while "simple methods like Keyword Stuffing traditionally used in SEO don't perform well." The best methods improved on baseline by **41% and 28%** on the two headline metrics.

The paper also reports a **democratizing effect** that matters for a small site. Table 2 breaks visibility gain down by the source's classic search rank: for rank-1 sources the effect was *negative* (Cite Sources -30.3%, Quotation Addition -22.9%, Statistics Addition -20.6%), while for rank-5 sources it was enormous (Cite Sources **+115.1%**, Quotation Addition +99.7%, Statistics Addition +97.9%). Rank-3 and rank-4 sources saw clear gains too (+20.4% and +15.5% for Cite Sources).

Two cautions about reading this. First, the experiment modified content with an LLM against a synthetic generative engine (Google Search for retrieval, GPT-3.5-turbo for synthesis, top 5 sources, five random seeds) — it is a controlled benchmark, not a field observation of today's production systems. Second, the paper's own framing is that strategies are domain-specific: the top-performing categories are Statement/Facts/Law & Government for Cite Sources, People & Society/Explanation/History for Quotation Addition, and Law & Government/Debate/Opinion for Statistics Addition.

The real-world validation is the more relevant part. Re-running the same strategies on **Perplexity.ai** as a deployed generative engine, the best methods improved on baseline by **22% on Position-Adjusted Word Count and 37% on Subjective Impression** — and keyword stuffing again underperformed (Position-Adjusted Word Count of 21.9 against a 24.1 baseline).

### Translating the paper into technique

The strategy names are LLM-generated edits, but the underlying moves are ordinary editorial craft:

**Quotation Addition** — put a named, attributable, verbatim statement in the content, with its source. A specific quote from a named person or a directly cited document beats paraphrase, because the model can lift a quotation *with* its attribution intact, and because attribution is exactly what makes a sentence safe to reuse.

**Statistics Addition** — replace qualitative hedging with quantified claims. "Performance improved significantly" is unusable; "click-through fell 58% for position-1 pages" is liftable, checkable and memorable.

**Cite Sources** — link out to the primary source for every external claim. This serves the reader, creates a corroboration path for the model, and mirrors the `sameAs` entity pattern at the sentence level.

**Fluency Optimization and Easy-to-Understand** — clear, well-structured prose outperformed. Both are ~15-30% visibility lifts on their own in the paper, which is a strong argument against the widespread belief that AI search rewards keyword-stuffed or deliberately obscure content.

**Keyword Stuffing** — performed *worse* than the baseline on the primary metric in both the synthetic and the Perplexity experiments. This is the cleanest empirical rebuttal of "AEO means writing for the machine."

### Answer-first and chunking

Here the industry and Google disagree, and both are partially right.

Google's position: "**'Chunking' content**: There's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it. Google systems are able to understand the nuance of multiple topics on a page and show the relevant piece to users… There's no ideal page length, and in the end, make pages for your audience, not just for generative AI search." It also explicitly rejects writing for machines: "You don't need to write in a specific way just for generative AI search. AI systems can understand synonyms and general meanings" ([Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)).

That is a statement about *Google's* retrieval, which is built on the classic Search index and Google's own passage understanding — a system that already indexes and ranks whole documents and selects passages at query time.

For engines that build their own retrieval over raw HTML, the mechanics are different, and Anthropic's engineering work shows why: traditional chunking "often results in the system failing to retrieve the relevant information" because chunks "lack sufficient context," and the fix is to prepend context to every chunk before indexing ([Anthropic, Contextual Retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/research/contextual-retrieval)). If your page is fetched as raw HTML and chunked by someone else's pipeline, the same failure mode applies to you: a section header that only makes sense in the context of the page title, a statistic without its unit or population, or a claim without its subject, will not retrieve.

**The synthesis that satisfies both:** you do not need to rewrite your content into chunks. You do need every section to be self-contained — to name its subject, state its unit, state its date, and stand alone if quoted out of context. That is good writing for humans *and* the precondition for retrieval by anyone else's pipeline. It is also, not coincidentally, what the answer-first pattern is for.

### The answer-first pattern

Put the answer in the first 40-60 words, then support it. This serves four audiences at once:

- A human skimming a SERP snippet.
- A model extracting the passage to quote.
- A retrieval pipeline scoring the chunk against the query.
- A reader who arrived from a citation and wants the conclusion immediately.

The structure that follows from it:

```markdown
## How many pages does Google consult for an AI Overview?

AI Overviews consult about 8.6 pages per query on average, versus 4.1 for
GPT-Search and 0.4 for GPT-4o with search tools enabled. On average, 53% of
the domains an AI Overview consults are not in the top 10 organic results for
the same query.

Source: arXiv:2510.11560, "Characterizing Web Search in the Age of
Generative AI" (2025).
```

The heading is a question because questions are how people ask. The first paragraph is a complete, quotable, self-contained answer with its numbers. The source is named inline so the claim can be traced — which is what "cite sources" means in practice.

### The quotable-statistics checklist

For each claim you want a model to lift, ask:

1. **Is there a number?** A claim without a number is a claim without a reason to be selected.
2. **Does the number have a unit, a population and a date?** "58%" alone is ambiguous; "58% lower CTR for position-1 pages, Ahrefs, December 2025 data" is usable.
3. **Is the number adjacent to its subject in the text?** Not in a footnote, not three paragraphs later.
4. **Can the source be named inline?** "per Pew Research Center's March 2025 panel" is quotable; "studies show" is not.
5. **Is the sentence independently parseable?** Read it alone. If it needs the previous paragraph, it will not survive extraction.
6. **Is the claim stable enough that you would defend it in six months?** A number you would not repeat is a liability when a model cites it.

## How do you build an entity that answer engines recognise?

Entity presence is the least technical and most under-invested part of AI search, and the data is unusually clear about what matters.

Ahrefs analysed **75,000 brands** against millions of AI Overview responses, using Spearman correlation to find which search factors correspond with brand visibility in AI Overviews ([An Analysis of AI Overview Brand Visibility Factors](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overview-brand-correlation)):

| Factor | Spearman correlation with AI Overview brand visibility |
| --- | --- |
| Branded web mentions (anywhere on the web, linked or unlinked) | **0.664** |
| Branded anchors (brand name in hyperlink text) | 0.527 |
| Branded search volume | 0.392 |
| Domain Rating | 0.326 |
| Referring domains | 0.295 |
| Branded organic traffic | 0.274 |
| Backlinks | 0.218 |
| Branded ad traffic | 0.216 |
| Branded ad cost | 0.215 |

The top three correlations are all **off-site** factors. Brand mentions correlate with AI Overview visibility roughly three times as strongly as backlinks do. And the distributional effect is stark: brands in the top quartile of web mentions earn **up to 10x more AI Overview mentions** than the next quartile — while **26% of brands have zero AI Overview mentions at all**.

Ahrefs' own caveat is the right one to carry: "correlation ≠ causation." But the direction is consistent with how the underlying models work — a language model's understanding of an entity comes from how often and in what contexts the name appears in text, which is why unlinked mentions, useless for classic link-based ranking, matter disproportionately in generative retrieval.

Note also that Seer Interactive's independent research found the same pattern for ChatGPT mentions, with weak correlations for domain rank (0.25) and backlinks (0.10) and the strongest relationship coming from branded search keywords.

Google's guidance on this is more cautious than the correlation data. Seeking "inauthentic 'mentions'" — manufactured references — "isn't as helpful as it might seem," because "our core ranking systems focus on high-quality content while other systems block spam; our generative AI features depend on both" ([Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). So the goal is *earned* presence: be where your field's conversations happen, and be consistently identifiable.

**What this means operationally for an individual professional:**

- **Consistency beats volume.** The same name, the same title, the same one-line description on every profile — LinkedIn, GitHub, your own site, conference listings, company pages — so that co-occurrence in the corpus resolves to one entity rather than several.
- **Third-party pages are the retrieval surface.** Your own site is one page; the corpus is full of other people's pages. If the third-party description of you is thin or inconsistent, the model has less to ground on.
- **Claim the namespace.** A canonical URL, a stable handle, an email, a Wikidata item where appropriate, and a `sameAs` array that connects them.
- **Write about your named specialty.** Repeated, specific, attributable content on a narrow topic builds the co-occurrence signal that generic content never will.
- **Earn mentions where the question is genuinely asked.** Stack Overflow, Reddit, Hacker News, industry forums, GitHub issues. Answer the question properly, with sources; the mention follows the answer.

## How do you measure AI search performance?

There are four separate things to measure, and they use four different tools. Most teams try to use one dashboard for all four, which is why their numbers never reconcile.

### 1. Impressions in AI features (Google, official)

Google ships a dedicated **Generative AI performance report** in Search Console, rolled out worldwide on 31 August 2026. It reports impressions from **AI Overviews and AI Mode** — the number of times links to your site were shown in a generative AI feature ([Generative AI performance report (Search)](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139)).

What it gives you: dimensions for **Pages**, **Countries**, **Dates** and **Devices**; a search-type filter separating **Web: text-based** from **Web: multimodal** (Lens, Circle to Search, uploaded images, Chrome image search); and CSV export. Data is aggregated by property on the chart and by page for page-dimension rows. AI Overviews and AI Mode are also counted inside the standard Performance report under the Web search type ([AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)).

There is a parallel report for generative AI features in **Discover** ([Generative AI performance report (Discover)](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16983858)), also worldwide since 31 August 2026, with a different impression rule: the link must be scrolled into view, and only one impression is counted per result per session.

Two caveats that trip people up: the report only appears if your site has accumulated enough AI-feature impressions (if you have excluded yourself via the Search generative AI control, it will never appear), and **Search Labs experiments are excluded** because those are still in active development.

### 2. Referral traffic from AI engines

GA4 added a native **AI Assistant** default channel group on **13 May 2026**. Sessions whose referrer matches a recognized AI assistant are assigned medium `ai-assistant`, campaign `(ai-assistant)`, and channel group "AI Assistant" ([Google Analytics, *What's new*](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9164320)). Google's own copy names "chatbots like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude."

Do not stop there. The native channel covers only referrers Google recognises, and referrals still land in plain "Referral" for anything else. Two supplements:

- **A custom channel group with a regex** on the referrer hostnames you care about (`chatgpt.com`, `perplexity.ai`, `claude.ai`, `gemini.google.com`, `copilot.microsoft.com`), applied *before* the default group so you keep a separate line for AI referrals.
- **Server-log or CDN-level referrer analysis.** Cloudflare's AI Crawl Control surfaces "Top referrers — top domains sending AI-driven referral traffic to your site (for example, `chatgpt.com`, `perplexity.ai`)" and "Most popular paths" — the pages AI crawlers hit and the pages they send traffic to ([Cloudflare AI Crawl Control](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-crawl-control/features/analyze-ai-traffic/)). It also exposes per-crawler request volume and status codes, which tells you whether your robots.txt policy is actually being honoured.

For market context, SE Ranking's analysis of 100,000+ websites puts AI referral traffic at **0.32% of all website traffic in 2026**, up from 0.24% in 2025 and 0.02% in 2024 — a 16x increase in under two years, but roughly **1 in every 312 visits** ([SE Ranking](https://seranking.com/blog/ai-traffic-research-study)). The share by engine in 2026: ChatGPT **74.78%**, Gemini **11.56%**, Perplexity **7.23%**, Copilot **3.51%**, Claude **2.62%**. ChatGPT's share is *falling* from 79.74% in 2025 while its absolute traffic grew 27%, because Gemini grew 231% and Claude grew 320% year over year.

### 3. Mention and citation share (the hard one)

There is no official reporting surface for "how often am I named in ChatGPT's answers." You build it, and you must understand what it can and cannot tell you.

The practical method:

1. **Define a prompt panel.** 30-100 questions a real buyer or practitioner would ask, across three clusters: category ("what tools do X"), comparison ("X vs Y"), and brand ("is X any good / who makes X"). Fix the panel and change it rarely.
2. **Run it on each engine on a schedule.** Weekly at minimum. Run through the consumer product or an API where you control the model and can sample repeatedly, because answers vary run to run.
3. **Record four fields per run:** mentioned (yes/no), cited (yes/no), position in the answer, and the exact quoted text when cited.
4. **Report rates, not rankings.** Mention rate = prompts where you are mentioned ÷ prompts run. Citation rate = prompts where you are cited ÷ prompts run. Share of voice = your citations ÷ all tracked brands' citations for the same panel. Report a range across runs, not a point estimate.
5. **Log sentiment and framing**, because a mention that misrepresents your position is worse than no mention.

The methodological pitfalls are well documented and worth stating plainly, because most "AI visibility scores" published by vendors ignore them:

- **Answers are non-deterministic.** The same prompt yields different wording, sources and recommendations across runs, locations, accounts, models and search modes. A single-run snapshot is not a measurement.
- **Citation distributions are heavy-tailed.** A handful of sources take most citations; small changes in sample size swing the numbers.
- **Prompt panels are a choice, and a biased one.** Your 50 prompts are a hypothesis about what buyers ask. Share of voice is only meaningful relative to a stated, fixed panel and a stated, fixed competitor set.
- **Sampling is expensive.** Rigorous panels sample each prompt many times per model, with fresh sessions for consumer apps — which is why most self-serve tools are directional at best.

Treat any single "AI visibility score" as a prompt-engineered index. Use it to compare your own panel across time, not to compare against a vendor's cross-client benchmark.

### 4. Classic performance (do not stop doing this)

AI-feature impressions are reported *inside* the standard Search Console Performance report under Web search type, so your existing reporting still covers them ([AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). The metric that has genuinely changed is CTR, and you should track it **split by whether a query produced an AI Overview** if your tooling allows it — because blended CTR hides the effect.

### Measurement stack

| What you want to know | Tool | Metric | Cadence | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Am I visible in Google AI features? | Search Console Generative AI performance report | AI-feature impressions by page/country/device | Weekly | Only for Google; absent if impressions are too low |
| Do I appear in third-party answers? | Your own prompt panel | Mention rate, citation rate, share of voice | Weekly | Non-deterministic; fix the panel |
| Do those answers send traffic? | GA4 AI Assistant + custom regex channel; log/CDN referrers | Sessions, engaged sessions, conversions from AI referrers | Weekly | Native channel misses unrecognised referrers |
| Is my crawler policy being honoured? | Server logs; Cloudflare AI Crawl Control | Requests per bot, status codes, blocked-vs-allowed | Weekly | Expect up to 24h propagation after changes |
| Is classic search still working? | Search Console Performance | Impressions, clicks, CTR, position — segmented by query | Weekly/monthly | AI features are inside the Web search type |
| Is traffic quality holding? | GA4 + Search Console | Engagement time, conversions, revenue per session | Monthly | Compare AI-referred vs organic cohorts |

## What does not work

Google's own guide has a "mythbusting" section that is worth reading as a unit, because it names the exact set of tactics that vendors have been selling. These are things you do **not** need to do for Google AI features ([Google's guide to optimizing for generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)):

- **Create `llms.txt` files or other "special" markup.** "Google Search itself doesn't use them."
- **Chunk content into tiny pieces.** "There's no requirement… There's no ideal page length."
- **Rewrite content just for AI systems.** "AI systems can understand synonyms and general meanings of what someone is seeking."
- **Seek inauthentic mentions.** "Seeking inauthentic 'mentions' across the web isn't as helpful as it might seem."
- **Add special schema for AI.** "There's no special schema.org markup you need to add."
- **Mass-produce pages for fan-out queries.** Doing so "primarily to manipulate rankings or generative AI responses… violates Google's scaled content abuse spam policy."

And from the evidence in this guide, two more:

- **Keyword stuffing.** It performed *worse* than no optimization on the primary GEO metric, in both the synthetic benchmark and the Perplexity validation.
- **Watching position 1 as your success metric.** The position-1 CTR penalty of -58% is the largest in Ahrefs' table, and Seer's data shows citation, not position, is the variable that moves clicks per impression.

The pattern across all of these: the tactics that fail are the ones that optimise *for the retrieval mechanism* rather than *for the reader*. The ones that work — real specificity, quotable evidence, honest structure, a resolvable entity, an accessible DOM — improve the experience for the human who clicked through as well as the model that quoted you.

## A 90-day implementation plan

**Days 1-15 — eligibility and policy**

- Verify the site is indexed and snippet-eligible; confirm Googlebot is not blocked at the robots.txt, CDN or WAF layer.
- Implement the crawler policy from the table above, split by purpose.
- Confirm the Search generative AI control in Search Console is set to *include*.
- Validate structured data with the Rich Results Test; fix any content-not-visible violations.
- Establish the `Person`/`Organization` entity with a stable `@id` and a `sameAs` array; add the `@id` reference to every page.

**Days 16-40 — content craft**

- Pick 10 existing pages that answer your buyers' questions. Rewrite their openings answer-first, in 40-60 words, with numbers, units and dates.
- Add inline source attribution to every external claim; add at least one named, attributable quotation per page where one genuinely exists.
- Audit every section heading for self-containment: could this section be quoted without its neighbours and still be true?
- Convert the two or three tables you already have into real, parseable tables with header rows.

**Days 41-60 — machine legibility**

- Publish `/llms.txt` per spec v2; generate Markdown twins for every page and add `rel="alternate" type="text/markdown"` and `rel="describedby"` link relations.
- Run Lighthouse's Agentic Browsing category and fix what it flags: accessibility-tree gaps, layout shift, missing `llms.txt`.
- Audit the DOM for ghost elements, missing `role`/`tabindex` on interactive elements, unlabelled inputs, and unstable layout.

**Days 61-90 — measurement**

- Configure the GA4 AI Assistant channel and a custom regex channel group for AI referrers.
- Build the prompt panel: 30-100 questions, three clusters, fixed competitor set. Record mentions, citations, positions and quoted text weekly from day one so you have a baseline before you change anything.
- Set up a monthly review that reads Search Console's Generative AI performance report next to the panel results and the referral data.
